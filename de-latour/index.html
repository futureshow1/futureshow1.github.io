Three ways of working

Almost everything I am asked for takes one of three shapes.

01

Training

Your team already has the material. It is the three or four incidents nobody brings up: the client who would not deal with a woman, the meeting where the interpreter softened something, the colleague who stopped speaking up in March. I collect them anonymously two weeks beforehand, and we spend the day on those instead of on cultural dimensions.

For

aid organisations, local government, schools, multinational teams

02

Facilitation

Some meetings cannot be chaired, only held. Consultations where the decision is already resented, a team meeting after a conflict, two sides at one table who each believe the other has already won. I prepare with each side separately, run the day to a written order, and afterwards write the note in language both sides can sign.

For

public institutions, organisations in crisis, teams after a change

03

Advisory

Your form is the first conversation your institution has with a person. Very often it is also the last. I go through the materials, the language, the procedures and the whole path someone takes to reach you, and come back with a list of what turns people away and what can be changed inside a month.

For

public administration, universities, integration programmes, EU projects

What can be said here

Three movements, whether it is a two-day course or one difficult meeting.

01

Find out what cannot be said yet

Before anything is practised, I find out which sentences are currently impossible in this room, and why. It is rarely the topic in the brief.

02

Say one true thing on purpose

Not everything. One thing, said deliberately, in front of everyone, at a moment the group can survive. That is the whole technique, and it takes preparation.

03

Leave a rule behind, not a feeling

A workshop that ends in goodwill has changed nothing by Thursday. We leave with one sentence, one procedure or one change to a form — something that works when I am not there.

Who I am

For years my job was to carry other people's words across a language.

I worked as a journalist and a translator in Brussels, and as a sworn interpreter in courtrooms — where a sentence is not finished until someone has said it again in the other language and both sides accept that this is what was said. You learn quickly that the work is not vocabulary. It is knowing what a person meant, what they said instead, and which of the two to carry across.

Then I stopped describing Europe and started working inside it. In 2016 the Council of Europe asked me to research and edit a handbook on answering hate speech. I spent a year reading the worst things people say to each other, and the best replies anyone had found. The most useful thing I learned was not what to say back. It was when not to.

Since then I have worked with teams that receive refugees, city departments whose forms nobody returns, and organisations where something was said in a meeting two years ago and has not been mentioned since. I do not make the difference disappear. I make it workable.

You do not build peace by avoiding conflict. You build it by making a room safe enough to go through one.

The problem is rarely the difference itself. It is the hatred that grows around it, and the stories people tell themselves about each other to keep it fed. My work is not to make the difference disappear. It is to build enough safety in the room that people can finally cross it — together, and out loud.

It was the first time anyone said out loud what we had all known for a year.

What I do here

Separate conversations first. The room comes later.

I do not ask people to be nice. I do not ask them to stop being angry. I ask them to say one true thing, at a moment the group can survive hearing it.

So I do not start with a workshop — in this situation a workshop only confirms that certain things are not said here. I start with four to six one-to-one conversations, to hear what each person sees and to find out whether the team is ready to name it in front of everyone. Then one facilitated meeting, where the thing is finally said in a way you can work with afterwards.

A — Editorial

Lineage: priyaparker.com. Instrument Serif + Inter, hairline rules, asymmetric grid.

The idea
The first line names the client's problem, not the author's profession. Three ways of working, not twelve services. Nothing here needs a photograph to look finished.
What it sells
Time, sold in three formats at three prices. A visitor places themselves in one of them within ten seconds.
Why it works
The named frame “What can be said here” gives the client something to quote to their own boss. Without a name there is nothing to order.
Choose it if
The plan is to work alone, with organisations, with no appetite for publishing. Lowest upkeep: written once, it stands for a year.
Risk
It lives or dies on the first sentence. If it turns cautious — “I support organisations in building competence” — the whole layout collapses into a business card.

C — Dossier

Lineage: erinmeyer.com, restyled as an institutional document. Inter + IBM Plex Mono, black and one red.

The idea
Built like a report, because that is the register these buyers live in. Numbered sections, a data card, and the track record as a real table rather than a row of logos.
What it sells
Defensibility. Written not for the person with the problem but for the person who has to justify the choice to a committee.
Why it works
The Council of Europe handbook is free, translated and cited — the one item that a stranger can verify independently. Here it is the spine of the page, not a line in a CV.
Choose it if
The market is public institutions, universities and EU programmes — which is exactly where the practice already is.
Risk
Cold. A small organisation may bounce off it. Every number has to survive a follow-up question, so “600 people” and “380 participants” need confirmation before publication.

B — Questions

Lineage: “Why are you here?” from mbs.works plus Perel's topic entry. Fraunces + Inter, warm ground, paper grain.

The idea
The page opens with no introduction. It asks “What are you dealing with?” Four situations as cards; each opens a real answer with a three-step flow, duration and group size.
What it sells
Relief. The visitor sees their own mess described in their own words and knows immediately that someone has done this before.
Why it works
It solves what A and B leave alone: very different buyers on one page. A school, a city department and a company enter by the same door and land in different places.
Choose it if
The site should qualify enquiries on its own. Closest to the logic of the HUMAN app's “Before the conversation”.
Risk
The most editorial work. The four situations have to be written precisely enough that people think “that is us”. Written loosely they read like a marketing quiz.

Which direction — and whose name

The three layouts differ not in decoration but in who stands at the centre: A the practice, B the institutional proof, C the client's situation. Styling can be changed in a week; this decision cannot.

On the name: Agata de Latour, not LIBRA

LIBRA is the name on the invoices since 2018, and it belongs in the footer as the registered practice. It should not be the name on the door. A trainer sells a person: what verifies is “Agata de Latour, Council of Europe”, not an abstract word. “Libra” also reads as a star sign in English and as Facebook's failed currency, and means nothing at all in French or Polish — while “de Latour” is distinctive and memorable in all three markets.

Domain in these drafts: agatadelatour.com, with libra kept only for invoicing.

Recommendation

Start with A, and move one element across from C: the institution strip and the Council of Europe handbook high on the page, not buried in “About”. That is one page, buildable in two weeks, which does not go stale even if nothing is ever published.

B is stage two — once it is clear which four situations actually recur in enquiries. Guessing them now is the one mistake that actively costs work.

Before anything goes further, four things to settle:

  • The first sentence — is “what they keep avoiding” right, or should it be cooler? It sets the whole register.
  • The name of the frame — “What can be said here” is a proposal, not a decision. It has to be the author's own.
  • Launch language — these drafts are in English so you can both judge them. The live site should open in French, English second, Polish third.
  • Everything marked in amber — figures, the quote, notice period. Nothing amber gets published unconfirmed.
